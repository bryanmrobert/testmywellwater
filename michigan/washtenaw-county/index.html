Washtenaw County, MI well water testing: county health department bottles and prices

Short answer: Buy sample bottles at Washtenaw County Environmental Health, 705 N Zeeb Road, Ann Arbor (weekdays 8:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m., closed noon–1 p.m.), fill them at home and return them by 3 p.m. on a weekday. Since November 1, 2026, a total coliform (bacteria) test is $19, and nitrate, nitrite, arsenic and lead are $16 each. Call 734-222-3800.

USGS's 2015 county estimate put about 67,000 Washtenaw County residents, roughly 19 percent of the population, on self-supplied domestic water; USGS says the most common source of self-supplied water is a well. [source] Washtenaw County Public Health said in 2015 that approximately 40,000 homes in the county are served by onsite water and sewage systems (private wells and septic systems). [source] The county says that if you don't get a water bill, chances are you have a private well, and that while safe, adequate water can be obtained in most of the county, water quality and quantity can be an issue in some areas. [source]

The Washtenaw County Health Department's Environmental Health Division sells sample bottles for private well tests. You collect the sample at home and bring it back to the office, and the county's contract lab runs the analysis. [source]

What and how often to test

The county says to test your well for bacteria every year, and also when the water's taste, look or smell suddenly changes, the water turns cloudy after rain or the well top floods, a contamination source such as a septic system or barnyard is within 50 feet, family members have unexplained flu-like symptoms, or the water system has been serviced or changed. [source] It suggests nitrate as a good, inexpensive first test if you're concerned about your well's vulnerability, and yearly arsenic testing if you have an arsenic treatment unit or earlier tests detected any arsenic. [source]

Statewide, MDHHS recommends testing annually for coliform bacteria and E. coli, nitrate and nitrite; every 3 to 5 years for arsenic and lead; and at least once for PFAS. [source] See our nitrate guide and coliform guide.

Arsenic, nitrate and lead in Washtenaw County

Arsenic occurs naturally in some areas of the county. The recommended level is less than 0.010 mg/L (10 ppb). [source]

Nitrate usually comes from man-made sources such as fertilizer, feedlots and sewage. In Washtenaw County it's most often found in denser developed areas (likely from septic systems) or rural areas with heavy farming, and it's more likely in shallower or sandy aquifers. The recommended level is less than 10 mg/L. [source]

Lead: the county says its drinking water sources don't have high levels of naturally occurring lead, but lead can leach from lead pipe and solder, more likely in homes built before 1986, and from faucets sold before 2014. [source]

The county also notes that a rotten-egg odor can mean hydrogen sulfide gas, an issue with some wells in the southeast part of the county, or iron bacteria. [source]

Local issue: the Gelman 1,4-dioxane plume

From 1966 to 1986, Gelman Sciences on South Wagner Road in Ann Arbor used 1,4-dioxane in manufacturing, and wastewater in unlined lagoons seeped into groundwater, forming a plume under eastern Scio Township and western Ann Arbor. It was found in residential wells in 1985, and in March 2026 EPA added the site to the Superfund National Priorities List. [source] Michigan's residential drinking water criterion for 1,4-dioxane is 7.2 ppb; there's no federal drinking water standard. [source]

Under a state contract, the county health department monitors drinking water wells around the plume, and about 500 wells have been sampled since 2014. As of 2026, 69 residential wells have detections, mostly at 1 ppb or less, and new detections farther north in Scio Township have been found since 2021. If your well isn't already in that monitoring and you want it tested, you pay the cost. The county lists Ann Arbor Technical Services (734-995-0995), the EGLE Drinking Water Laboratory (517-335-8184) and SimpleLab as labs that test for 1,4-dioxane. Only EGLE sends copies of results to the county; for other labs, the county asks you to email Jennifer Conn (connj@washtenaw.org, 734-222-3855) if you want your results kept on file. She can also review your well record and the depth to contaminated groundwater near you. [source] EGLE's fee for a 1,4-dioxane test is $169. [source]

Buying or selling a home with a well

Washtenaw County's Time of Sale regulation requires residential onsite well and septic systems to be inspected before a home can be sold. Sellers hire a county-certified inspector, and since approvals are valid for one year, the county recommends doing the inspection before the home is listed. [source] Homes on municipal water and sewer aren't affected; Environmental Health reviews reports within 5 business days and must authorize the sale before the deed can transfer. [source] The inspection report includes bacteria and nitrate test results, run by a state-certified lab, plus other water quality tests the county requires. [source] The county requires coliform, nitrate and arsenic tests for all residential wells of homes being sold. [source]

If coliform is found

The county says not to drink the water until you get a safe (bacteria absent) result; you can still bathe, do laundry and clean with it. Use bottled water for drinking, cooking, baby formula and brushing teeth, or boil the water for 1 minute and let it cool. The whole system must be flushed and disinfected; a homeowner may do it, but the county recommends a registered well driller, who can also find structural problems. Hold the chlorinated water in the well and pipes for at least 8 hours, then flush it through an outdoor hose onto the yard, away from the septic system. Retest before drinking. [source]

New wells

A new or replacement private residential well permit is $358. [source] Coliform, nitrate and arsenic testing is required for all new and replacement wells. [source]

State lab and other certified labs

The county doesn't run partial chemistry (calcium, chloride, fluoride, hardness, iron, magnesium, nitrates, nitrites, sodium and sulfate). That panel goes through the EGLE Drinking Water Lab for $79 plus shipping; call 517-335-8184 to order. [source] EGLE's fee schedule, effective October 1, 2026, also lists total coliform and E. coli at $23, nitrate and nitrite at $41, PFAS at $320 and volatile organic compounds at $147. [source] [source] For other tests, search EGLE's laboratory certification lists. [source]

FAQ

Where can I get my well water tested in Washtenaw County, Michigan?

Buy sample bottles at Washtenaw County Environmental Health, 705 N Zeeb Road, Ann Arbor (weekdays 8:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m., closed noon–1 p.m.), fill them at home and return them by 3 p.m. on a weekday. Since November 1, 2026, a total coliform (bacteria) test is $19, and nitrate, nitrite, arsenic and lead are $16 each. Call 734-222-3800.

How much does well water testing cost in Washtenaw County, MI?

Since November 1, 2026, Washtenaw County Environmental Health charges $19 for a total coliform (bacteria) test and $16 each for nitrate, nitrite, arsenic and lead. Most other chemical tests are $10 to $16. Bottles bought before November 1 can still be analyzed at no extra charge.

Where do I get a well water test kit in Washtenaw County?

Buy sample bottles at the Environmental Health office, 705 N Zeeb Road, Ann Arbor, Monday to Friday 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. (closed noon to 1 p.m.), or call 734-222-3800 to pay by card and pick them up from the lobby table. Return filled bottles by 3 p.m. on a weekday.

Do I need a well inspection to sell a house in Washtenaw County?

Yes. The county's Time of Sale regulation requires onsite well and septic systems to be inspected by a county-certified inspector and approved before the home is sold, and wells of homes being sold must be tested for coliform bacteria, nitrate and arsenic. The county's report review fee is $124 for a well, or $187 for a well and septic system.

Should I test my Washtenaw County well for 1,4-dioxane?

If you live near the Gelman plume in Scio Township or western Ann Arbor and your well isn't already in the state-funded monitoring, you can test at your own cost. The county lists Ann Arbor Technical Services, the EGLE Drinking Water Laboratory and SimpleLab. Michigan's drinking water criterion is 7.2 ppb.
