Buying a house with a well: a testing checklist

  1. Check your state's law. New Jersey requires testing as a condition of sale under the PWTA, and closing can't happen until both parties have reviewed the results. [source] Virginia has no such regulation, though testing is part of a standard contract. [source]
  2. Ask your lender. VAHWQP notes lead and nitrate are required by some lenders. [source]
  3. Test the basics. Coliform and E. coli bacteria are standard practice in Virginia home sales. [source] EPA's yearly baseline is total coliform, nitrates, TDS and pH. [source]
  4. Use a certified lab. The EPA says to use labs certified for drinking water testing. [source]
  5. Get the well records. In Virginia, completion reports for wells built after 1990 are public records at local health departments. [source]
  6. Look around the property. EPA links nearby activities to tests. For example, intensive agriculture suggests nitrate, nitrite, pesticides and coliform, and gas drilling suggests chloride, sodium, barium and strontium. [source]
  7. Know what a failed test means. In NJ, a failed result doesn't by itself block the sale, but lenders or buyers may require treatment. [source]

This is a general checklist, not legal or real-estate advice. Ask your agent, attorney and lender about your transaction.

FAQ

Is a well water test required to buy a house?

It depends on the state. New Jersey requires it under the PWTA; VAHWQP says Virginia has no such regulation, though testing is part of a standard contract and some lenders require it.
