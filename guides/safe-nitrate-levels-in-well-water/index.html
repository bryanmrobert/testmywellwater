Safe nitrate levels in well water

Short answer: The EPA's legal limit for public drinking water is 10 milligrams per liter of nitrate measured as nitrogen. The EPA warns that infants under six months who drink water above that level could become seriously ill, with symptoms including shortness of breath and blue-baby syndrome. The CDC says all well owners should test for nitrates every year.

The number to know

The EPA's maximum contaminant level (MCL) for nitrate, measured as nitrogen, is 10 mg/L. Infants below six months who drink water above the MCL could become seriously ill and, if untreated, may die. Symptoms include shortness of breath and blue-baby syndrome. EPA lists sources as fertilizer runoff, leaking septic tanks and sewage, and erosion of natural deposits. [source]

The MCL is a legal limit for public water systems. EPA rules don't apply to private wells, but it's the standard benchmark for interpreting results. [source]

Where nitrate in wells comes from

The CDC lists animal waste, septic systems, wastewater, flooded sewers, polluted stormwater runoff, fertilizers, farm runoff and decaying plants. High nitrate can mean other harmful chemicals or germs are present, so ask your health department what else to test for. [source]

How often to test

The CDC says all well owners should test for nitrates at least once a year [source], and the EPA recommends testing for nitrate, nitrite, pesticides and coliform near intensive agriculture. [source] In Pennsylvania, Penn State's Agriculture/Septic package adds nitrate-nitrogen to the standard test for $70. [source]

If your result is high

The CDC says not to drink water contaminated with chemicals until it's treated, and notes that boiling won't remove chemicals. [source]
