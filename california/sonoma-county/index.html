Sonoma County, CA well water testing: county public health lab and certified labs

Short answer: Pick up a bottle at the Sonoma County Public Health Laboratory, 3313 Chanate Road, Santa Rosa, and return the sample in person Monday–Thursday, 8 a.m.–4 p.m. Its routine bacteria test (total coliform and E. coli) is $39. The lab doesn't test chemicals, so use a county-listed certified lab for nitrate. Call 707-565-4711.

Permit Sonoma says the county has roughly 40,000 water wells, the most per capita of any county in California, and that about 23,000 parcels rely on groundwater from a private well for their primary water supply. [source] USGS's 2015 county estimate put the self-supplied population at about 77,000, roughly 15% of residents; USGS says the most common source of self-supplied water is a well. [source]

Sonoma County Environmental Health says private well water isn't regulated by any government agency, so it's the property owner's job to have the well tested by a state-certified laboratory. Landlords are required to provide potable water to their tenants. [source]

County lab bacteria test: how it works

ItemDetails
Who runs itSonoma County Public Health Regional Laboratory, 3313 Chanate Road, Santa Rosa, CA 95404. Phone 707-565-4711; business hours Monday–Friday 8:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m. [source] The lab is accredited by California's Environmental Laboratory Accreditation Program (ELAP certificate #1736). [source]
What it testsBacteria only. The standard test is Colilert presence/absence for total coliforms and E. coli, which the lab says is what most private wells need; a Quantitray test gives a numerical count if you need one. The lab does not test for chemicals, metals, minerals or Giardia. [source] [source]
Getting a bottleBottles are in the lab lobby at 3313 Chanate Road. Only water collected in a bottle provided by the lab will be tested; check that your bottle hasn't expired. [source] [source]
Collecting the sampleUse a cold-water tap such as the kitchen faucet, remove the aerator, run the water 2–3 minutes, then fill to the 100 ml line without touching the inside of the cap or bottle. Collect on the day you drop off; if you can't deliver within an hour, keep it refrigerated or on an ice pack (not frozen). Samples more than 30 hours old are rejected. [source]
Dropping offIn person, Monday–Thursday 8:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m. Samples are not accepted on Fridays, weekends, holidays or the day before a holiday. Fill out a test requisition form (lab slip) for each sample. [source]
PayingCash, check or most credit cards. [source]
ResultsThe lab page says results are ready in about 24 hours; its collection instructions say testing takes 24–48 hours and private clients may expect emailed results in 2–3 days. [source] [source]

What and how often to test

TestCounty-recommended frequency
Bacteria (total coliform and fecal coliform)Twice a year, or after a flood or other disaster that may have affected the well
NitrateEvery five years
MineralsEvery five years, or if the water's taste or color changes
Petroleum, solvents and pesticidesEvery three to five years
VOCs and SVOCsAfter wildfire damage to the water system or suspected industrial contamination

The county says to install a treatment device or find another water supply if nitrate is above 45 mg/L as NO3 (10 mg/L as nitrate-nitrogen), and to look for and fix the cause if total coliform turns up. Questions: Sonoma County Environmental Health, 707-565-6565, weekdays 8 a.m.–4 p.m. [source] See our nitrate guide and coliform guide.

Local issues: wildfire and PFAS

After a wildfire: the county says that if water pipes, well casings or other plastic parts were exposed to high heat, the system lost pressure, or wildfire debris got into it, the water should be tested for volatile and semi-volatile organic compounds (VOCs and SVOCs). [source]

PFAS: the county says PFAS testing needs specialized collection because samples are easily contaminated by common household sources, so well owners may want to work with experienced environmental professionals or trained sample collectors, and have the sample analyzed by an accredited lab. [source] [source]

New wells and permits

The County of Sonoma requires a permit to drill, deepen or abandon a groundwater well, under the county well ordinance (County Code Chapter 25B). Permit Sonoma notes that well permitting has been the subject of litigation and posts status updates on its well ordinance page. [source] For wells and septic systems, contact Permit Sonoma Well and Septic at 707-565-2849. [source]

FAQ

Where can I get my well water tested in Sonoma County, California?

Pick up a bottle at the Sonoma County Public Health Laboratory, 3313 Chanate Road, Santa Rosa, and return the sample in person Monday–Thursday, 8 a.m.–4 p.m. Its routine bacteria test (total coliform and E. coli) is $39. The lab doesn't test chemicals, so use a county-listed certified lab for nitrate. Call 707-565-4711.

How much does a well water test cost in Sonoma County, CA?

The Sonoma County Public Health Laboratory's FY 2026-27 fee schedule lists the routine Colilert presence/absence test for total coliform and E. coli at $39. A Quantitray test that counts E. coli is $51.50 undiluted or $52.53 diluted.

Where do I drop off a well water sample in Sonoma County?

Pick up a bottle in the lobby of the Public Health Laboratory, 3313 Chanate Road, Santa Rosa, and return the sample in person Monday through Thursday, 8 a.m. to 4 p.m. Samples aren't accepted on Fridays, weekends, holidays or the day before a holiday, or if they're more than 30 hours old.

Does the Sonoma County lab test well water for nitrate or arsenic?

No. The county Public Health Laboratory tests for bacteria only and does not test for chemicals, metals or minerals. Sonoma County Environmental Health lists ELAP-accredited labs for chemical testing, including Brelje and Race and K Prime in Santa Rosa.

How often should I test my well in Sonoma County?

Sonoma County Environmental Health recommends bacteria testing twice a year or after a flood or disaster, nitrate and minerals every five years, and petroleum, solvents and pesticides every three to five years.

How many private wells are in Sonoma County?

Permit Sonoma says the county has roughly 40,000 water wells, the most per capita in California, and about 23,000 parcels rely on a private well for their primary water supply.
