Licking County, OH well water testing: health department sampling

Short answer: Fill out the Licking County Health Department's online water sample request form, and the office will contact you, usually within one business day, to set up a visit to sample your well. A bacteria test (total coliform and E. coli) is $30 plus a $35 trip charge, and results usually come by email in 3 to 4 business days.

USGS's 2015 county estimate put about 71,000 Licking County residents, roughly 41 percent of the population, on self-supplied domestic water. [source] Ohio EPA's Central Ohio Regional Water Study gives a similar picture: it estimates the county's private water systems (well users) draw about 4.5 million gallons a day, figured at 70 gallons per person per day, which works out to roughly 64,000 people. [source]

In Ohio, private water systems, including wells, cisterns, hauled water storage tanks and ponds used for drinking water, are regulated under the Ohio Private Water Systems rules (Ohio Administrative Code 3701-28), and the Licking County Health Department samples both new and existing private water systems. [source]

What and how often to test

Ohio State University Extension says a private well should be tested at least once a year for total coliform bacteria, nitrate, pH and total dissolved solids or conductivity. [source] The county's test menu covers bacteria and nitrate/nitrite, plus arsenic, lead, fluoride and iron; for pH, total dissolved solids or anything else, use another certified lab. See our coliform guide and nitrate guide.

Nitrate, lead and arsenic

Nitrate: Ohio's private water system standard for nitrate is 10 ppm (Ohio Administrative Code 3701-28-04), and all new and altered wells have been pre-screened and tested for nitrate since 2000. The Ohio Department of Health says infant formula should be made with safe bottled water when nitrate is higher than 10 ppm. [source]

Lead: ODH says lead is relatively uncommon in the groundwater supplying Ohio wells; it more often comes from plumbing, brass fixtures and older well parts, and homes built before 1986 are more likely to have lead pipes, fixtures and solder. ODH recommends acting to remove or reduce lead when levels are above 15 ppb, and notes that boiling won't remove lead. [source]

Arsenic: Ohio EPA says most arsenic in Ohio's ground water is naturally occurring and higher levels are found more often in ground water than surface water. The public water limit is 10 micrograms per liter (10 ppb), and Ohio EPA tells private well owners to contact their local health department about arsenic levels in their area or about testing. [source]

Local groundwater

ODNR's ground water pollution potential report for the county says buried valleys in western Licking County are filled mostly with fine-grained till and lake deposits, where thin sand and gravel lenses make poor, low-yielding aquifers with very low vulnerability. Buried valleys in central and eastern Licking County often hold thick sand and gravel outwash, high-yielding aquifers that tend to be highly vulnerable to contamination. Shales, siltstones and sandstones are the main aquifer in much of central and eastern Licking County. [source] That report dates to 1995, so use it for general setting, not your own well.

Former Newark Air Force Base: the Air Force Civil Engineer Center held an open house on October 15, 2026 about planned private drinking water well sampling to assess whether PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are affecting groundwater used for drinking water around the former installation. [source] The county's standard test menu doesn't include PFAS. [source]

If coliform is found

ODH says to disinfect a well after a coliform-positive presence/absence result, a total coliform result above four colony-forming units, or any E. coli, and that a homeowner may do it on an existing well. Its steps: pump the well heavily to waste (not into the septic system), work out the volume of water in the casing from your well log, add a chlorine and white vinegar solution, recirculate it with a garden hose, run every faucet until you smell chlorine, let it sit 8 to 24 hours, then flush. Wait a few days and have the local health district collect another total coliform sample, making sure no chlorine is left in the water. If coliform or E. coli persists, call a registered private water systems contractor. [source]

New wells and repairs

A permit from the Health Department is required before installing or altering a private water system, and well logs may also be available from the Ohio Department of Natural Resources. [source]

Other certified labs

For PFAS, pH, total dissolved solids or other tests, use a laboratory on Ohio EPA's combined list of certified drinking water labs. [source]

FAQ

Where can I get my well water tested in Licking County, Ohio?

Fill out the Licking County Health Department's online water sample request form, and the office will contact you, usually within one business day, to set up a visit to sample your well. A bacteria test (total coliform and E. coli) is $30 plus a $35 trip charge, and results usually come by email in 3 to 4 business days.

How much does well water testing cost in Licking County, OH?

The Licking County Health Department charges $30 for a bacteria test (total coliform and E. coli), $68 for nitrates/nitrites, $27 each for arsenic and lead, $26 for fluoride and $14 for iron. A $35 trip charge is added, so a bacteria test comes to $65.

How do I get my well tested by the Licking County Health Department?

Fill out the department's online water sample request form. The office will contact you, usually within one business day, to confirm an appointment, and staff take the sample. Sampling is done Monday to Wednesday from noon to 4 p.m. and Thursday from 10 a.m. to 2:30 p.m.

How long do Licking County well test results take?

The Health Department says results normally come back within 3 to 4 business days after the sample is taken, though some tests take longer. Results are emailed unless you ask for another method.

How many people in Licking County use private wells?

USGS's 2015 estimate put about 71,000 residents, roughly 41 percent, on self-supplied domestic water. Ohio EPA's Central Ohio Regional Water Study estimates private water systems serve roughly 64,000 people, based on 4.5 million gallons a day at 70 gallons per person.
