McHenry County, IL well water testing: the county health department lab and township drop-offs

Short answer: Get a bottle and form from the McHenry County Department of Health, 667 Ware Road, Woodstock, or a participating township office. Its $30 test covers total coliform, E. coli and nitrates. Drop off samples the day you collect them: Woodstock, Monday–Thursday 8 a.m.–2 p.m.; townships, Wednesdays 9 a.m.–noon. Call 815-334-4585.

McHenry County gets 100 percent of its drinking water, private and public, from groundwater. [source] USGS's 2015 county estimate put about 66,000 residents, roughly 21 percent of the population, on self-supplied domestic water; USGS says the most common source of self-supplied water is a well. [source] A USGS study of the county's sand and gravel aquifers counted about 72,000 people on self-supplied domestic wells in 2010 and notes that the western half of the county generally uses private wells and septic systems, while the eastern half is mostly on municipal water. [source] The county's Water Resource Specialist has said there are still between 30,000 and 50,000 private wells in the area relying on the sand and gravel aquifer. [source]

The McHenry County Department of Health encourages private well owners to test every year for at least total coliform bacteria, E. coli and nitrates, and to test again if the water quality changes or the well floods. Its Division of Environmental Health runs its own lab for that test. [source]

When to test

The department says to test your well every year, and also if anyone in the home has recurring stomach (gastrointestinal) illness; if you're pregnant, planning a pregnancy or have a baby under one year old; after heavy rain or flooding; after the well is repaired; and again after the well has been disinfected or chlorinated. Warning signs of a well problem include a pump that cycles constantly, a drop in water pressure, a strange odor or taste, particles in the water and a damaged casing. [source] It also reminds owners not to assume their water is safe just because a neighbor's is. [source]

Testing for metals, solvents, pesticides, herbicides and petroleum products isn't routinely done on a home water supply, the department says, but it has information on their health effects. [source]

How to collect the sample

Follow the instructions that come with the county bottle. IDPH's general advice: sample from a tap as close to the well as possible, not a garden hose, dirty faucet or swing-type faucet, and remove the aerator. Run the water at full flow until the pump starts and runs for several minutes, then turn it down to a pencil-sized stream. Don't rinse the bottle or touch the rim or inside of the cap, fill it to the fill line and cap it right away. [source]

Nitrate

The department says high nitrate levels may be dangerous to pregnant women and children under one, and that boiling water does not remove nitrates. [source] IDPH says levels above 10 mg/L (as nitrogen) may cause illness in infants 6 months old or younger, and that nitrate in groundwater usually comes from agricultural fertilizers, livestock wastes or septic systems. [source] See our nitrate guide.

Arsenic, chloride and other local water quality issues

USGS sampled the county's sand and gravel aquifers through a network of monitoring wells in 2010. Health-based standards were exceeded for arsenic in 22 percent of the monitoring wells sampled, sodium in 20 percent and nitrate in 2 percent. Aesthetic standards were exceeded for iron in 85 percent, total dissolved solids in 33 percent, manganese in 30 percent and chloride in 11 percent. USGS says arsenic, iron and manganese occur naturally but are elevated where groundwater has low oxygen. [source]

USGS also found elevated chloride and sodium linked in part to road salt; its source analysis points to a mix of road salt, water softeners and sewage. In three of six wells resampled in 2015, chloride had risen by as much as 521 percent since a 1979 study, and shallow wells near urban areas showed trace volatile organic compounds and pesticides. [source]

Arsenic has no smell or taste, so testing is the only way to find it. IDPH uses EPA's 10 parts per billion public-water standard as the guideline for private wells, recommends an Illinois EPA-certified lab for arsenic testing, and says that if levels are high you should stop using the water for drinking, cooking and baby formula; bathing and laundry are fine. A treatment system meeting ANSI/NSF Standard 53 can reduce arsenic. [source] The county lab sends arsenic out to a private lab for $55. [source]

If coliform is found

The department says not to drink the water if the sample shows coliform bacteria. [source] It recommends a qualified water well professional for disinfection, especially for driven point wells and wells in pits (pit wells should be disinfected by licensed contractors), but owners can disinfect their own drilled well. [source]

  1. Work out how much water is in the well: gallons per foot (0.65 for a 4-inch well, 1.5 for a 6-inch well) times the feet of water in the well.
  2. For each 100 gallons, mix 3 cups of laundry bleach (5.25% chlorine) into about 10 gallons of water and pour it into the top of the well.
  3. Run a hose from an outside spigot back into the well for about 15 minutes, then open each hot and cold faucet until you smell chlorine.
  4. Let the system stand for at least several hours, preferably overnight, then run each faucet until the chlorine odor is gone, keeping the flow low if you're on a septic system and directing outdoor discharge away from it.
  5. When you submit a follow-up sample, tell lab staff the well was disinfected. They'll check for leftover chlorine, which could invalidate the bacteria test.

Steps summarized from the county's disinfection sheet; keep children and pets away, wear eye protection and gloves, and never mix bleach with other chemicals. [source] Questions: 815-334-4585. See our coliform guide.

After a flood

Don't use a well whose wellhead has been under floodwater until the water recedes; treat the supply as contaminated until a sample shows no coliform bacteria. Use bottled water or bring tap water to a rolling boil for at least five minutes. After the floodwater recedes and the well has been flushed and disinfected, the department tests flooded private wells for coliform bacteria for free; call first to check bottle availability, then drop the sample off Monday to Thursday, 8 a.m.–2 p.m., with a completed chain of custody form. Samples collected more than 24 hours before drop-off can't be tested. [source]

Protecting your well

The department says a well should be at least 50 feet from a septic tank and 75 feet from a conventional septic system's distribution lines, the well cap should be at least 8 inches above ground and tightly secured and screened, and the ground should slope away from the well. Don't store chemicals near the well, and properly seal abandoned wells. Call the department if your well is in a pit or the wellhead is buried. [source] Its homeowner brochure includes a page for keeping a record of water tests and well maintenance. [source]

Other labs and help with results

Other IDPH-certified labs in and around McHenry County also test private well water. [source] For metals, VOCs or other tests the county lab doesn't run, use an Illinois EPA accredited lab. [source] IDPH interprets test results and gives recommendations for free. [source]

FAQ

Where can I get my well water tested in McHenry County, Illinois?

Get a bottle and form from the McHenry County Department of Health, 667 Ware Road, Woodstock, or a participating township office. Its $30 test covers total coliform, E. coli and nitrates. Drop off samples the day you collect them: Woodstock, Monday–Thursday 8 a.m.–2 p.m.; townships, Wednesdays 9 a.m.–noon. Call 815-334-4585.

How much does well water testing cost in McHenry County, IL?

The McHenry County Department of Health lab charges $30 for its private well test covering total coliform bacteria, E. coli and nitrates. Its fee table also lists a $10 nitrate screen, $23 quantitative nitrate and $55 arsenic (both sent to a private lab). Pay by card or check; cash isn't accepted.

Where do I get a well water test kit in McHenry County?

Pick up the department's sample bottle and chain of custody form at the Division of Environmental Health, 667 Ware Road, Woodstock, or at the Algonquin, Grafton, Marengo, McHenry or Nunda township offices during business hours.

When can I drop off a well water sample in McHenry County?

Collect the sample the same day you turn it in. The Woodstock lab accepts samples Monday through Thursday, 8 a.m. to 2 p.m.; participating township offices accept them Wednesdays from 9 a.m. to noon (Grafton from 7:30 a.m.). Results are available the next business day.

Does McHenry County test flooded wells for free?

Yes. After floodwater recedes and the well has been flushed and disinfected, the McHenry County Department of Health tests flooded private wells for coliform bacteria at no charge. Call 815-334-4585 first to check bottle availability.
