DuPage County, IL well water testing: the county health department lab

Short answer: Pick up a bottle and instructions at a DuPage County Health Department Public Health Center in Addison, Lombard, Westmont or Wheaton, collect the sample in the morning, and drop it off at a Public Health Center before noon the same day, Monday through Thursday. The county lab charges $33 for coliform and $16 for a nitrate screen.

About 20,000 homes and 200 businesses in DuPage County are served by water wells, according to the DuPage County Health Department. [source] Much of the county drinks Lake Michigan water, but the department notes that many people throughout the county still drink water from local underground aquifers. [source] USGS's 2015 county estimate put the self-supplied population at about 73,000; the county's home count suggests the real number of well users is lower. [source] [source]

If you live outside a municipality or in an area without public water, your water almost certainly comes from a private well. In DuPage County these wells typically tap water-bearing limestone formations 100 to 200 feet below the surface. [source] The Health Department encourages well owners to test every year. [source]

How to collect the sample

  1. Fill in the Potable Water Sample Report in black ink, including the date and time collected, and check the tests you want.
  2. Sample from a kitchen or washroom faucet after removing any aerator. Wash your hands before opening the bottle.
  3. Let the cold water run for at least three minutes.
  4. Fill the bottle above the 100 mL fill line, up to the neck. The lab can't test samples with less than 100 mL. Reseal the lid and attach the form.
  5. Keep the sample cool and take it straight to the nearest Public Health Center before noon.

Steps summarized from the county's sample form. [source]

When to test

The Health Department recommends testing private well water for coliform bacteria every year, and whenever work is done on the well or plumbing, such as replacing a hot water tank. Test more than once a year if household members or visitors have recurring stomach or intestinal illness, or if the water's taste, odor or appearance changes. [source]

Reading the results

The report marks a sample satisfactory when it meets Illinois EPA and DuPage County standards with no coliform bacteria. A nitrate screen under 1 ppm is satisfactory. A screen at 1 ppm or more may be unsatisfactory (above 10 ppm is unsatisfactory), and the county recommends confirming it with quantitative testing on a new sample by a certified lab. [source] IDPH says not to boil nitrate-contaminated water, because boiling concentrates nitrate. [source] See our nitrate guide.

The county's coliform and nitrate tests don't check for metals, pesticides or volatile organic compounds. For those, the form says to use a laboratory certified for the additional testing. [source]

If coliform is found

A well contractor can disinfect your well, or you can do it yourself with one to two gallons of regular, unscented chlorine bleach for the average home well, following the county's steps: check the well cap and casing, pour a bleach solution into the well, recirculate with a garden hose, run each faucet until you smell bleach, let it stand overnight, then flush. Use the water for two days and arrange a new test once the bleach taste and odor are gone. Until you get a satisfactory result, boil drinking water or use bottled water. [source] The county's homeowner guide also recommends testing again about two weeks after chlorinating, and says to disinfect any time the well is serviced. [source] See our coliform guide.

Hard water, iron and odors

The county says calcium and magnesium, the minerals that cause hardness, are common in well water throughout DuPage County, and iron adds to it; a water softener is the economical fix. For iron at 0.3–1.0 mg/L a softener should help, and above 1.0 mg/L the county suggests an oxidizing (catalyst) filter ahead of the softener. Red slime in toilet tanks comes from harmless iron bacteria, and a rotten-egg smell comes from hydrogen sulfide made by sulfur bacteria; chlorinating the system may help but isn't a permanent fix. [source] If you're on a salt-restricted diet, the county says to ask your doctor before using a softener. [source]

Connecting to city water: seal or keep the well

Illinois and DuPage County law require an abandoned well to be properly sealed within 30 days of connecting to a public water supply or of notice from the Health Department. A sealing application and permit fee must be paid before the sealing is scheduled, and the driller sends the department an affidavit when the work is done. [source] The county's permit to seal a well is $171. [source]

If your water supplier allows it, you can apply to keep the well as a supplemental well for uses like watering the lawn, filling a pool or washing the car. The Health Department inspects it, you add approved backflow protection, and you record a Notice of Supplemental Well with the County Recorder of Deeds. Otherwise the well must be sealed by an Illinois licensed water well contractor, or by the owner-occupant with the department's prior approval. [source] For new well or well repair permits, call Plan Review at 630-221-7045. [source]

Other labs

For metals, VOCs or other tests the county lab doesn't run, use an Illinois EPA accredited lab. [source] IDPH interprets test results and gives recommendations for free. [source]
